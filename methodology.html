Analytical Framework

Methodology and Limitations

Environmental data analysis carries an obligation of procedural disclosure. This page documents the mathematical definitions, statutory validity thresholds, sensor hiatuses, and modeling constraints governing investigations published on Signal Earth.

01 : Regulatory Formula

CPCB National Air Quality Index (NAQI) Mathematical Formulation

The National Air Quality Index developed by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB, October 2014) utilizes a linear piecewise sub-index formulation. For any pollutant parameter p with observed 24-hour average concentration Cp, the sub-index Ip is computed as:

Ip = [ (IHI - ILO) / (BHI - BLO) ] * (Cp - BLO) + ILO

Where BHI and BLO represent the upper and lower breakpoint concentrations for the category containing Cp, and IHI and ILO represent the corresponding sub-index scale bounds. The composite daily AQI is determined by the maximum sub-index across all monitored criteria pollutants:

AQI = max(IPM2.5, IPM10, INO2, ISO2, ICO, IO3, INH3, IPb)

The pollutant responsible for max(Ip) is designated as the Dominant Pollutant for that monitoring day.

02 : Statutory Compliance

Official versus Indicative Validity Rules

Under statutory CPCB guidelines, a daily AQI score is officially valid only when all of the following conditions are satisfied:

  1. Criteria Pollutant Quorum: At least three criteria pollutants must have valid daily sub-indices.
  2. Particulate Presence: At least one of the valid pollutants must be either PM2.5 or PM10.
  3. Temporal Completeness: Each pollutant parameter must have a minimum of 16 valid hourly readings within the 24-hour observation cycle (at least 66.7% data completeness).
Validation Accounting in Our Pipeline

Many informal dashboards report an "AQI" whenever any single parameter (such as PM2.5 alone) is active. In our Delhi/Alipur pipeline, across 1,825 total calendar rows, 1,137 days satisfy the full statutory criteria (62.3%), while 688 days are partial or missing. We partition these into two explicit fields: AQI_Official (valid regulatory days) and AQI_Indicative (single-parameter estimates). We do not present an indicative value as official CPCB data.

03 : Reporting Hiatuses

Instrument Drops and Missing Intervals

Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Stations (CAAQMS) are mechanical instruments operating in high particulate concentrations. They experience beta-attenuation tape depletion, optical recalibration, and sensor outages:

  • Delhi / Alipur (CPCB/DPCC Site 204): An operational hiatus occurred from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2020 (732 consecutive days) where criteria pollutants went unrecorded due to station refurbishment. Rather than interpolating across a two-year void, our pipeline preserves this interval as missing data.
  • Kanpur / Kalyanpur and Nehru Nagar (UPPCB/CPCB): Observations ingested via OpenAQ v3 span July 2021 through October 2022, followed by an unmonitored hiatus until early 2025. Data from these stations is analyzed strictly within its verified observation windows.

Signal Earth Principle: We do not impute or forward-fill long-duration instrument dropouts. Data gaps are reported as missing observations on all timelines and tables.

04 : Machine Learning Evaluation

Chronological Testing Splits

For day-ahead AQI time-series predictive modeling:

  • Short-Gap Interpolation: Linear interpolation is permitted strictly for short gaps of 1 to 2 consecutive days. Gaps of 3 or more days reset the lag sequence window.
  • Strict Out-of-Time Split: Standard random k-fold cross-validation causes temporal leakage in atmospheric time series because future weather states leak into past predictions. The model was trained exclusively on records prior to 2023 and evaluated strictly on the unobserved 2023 calendar year (Test MAE: 39.69 AQI points, RMSE: 54.98 AQI points, R² = 0.769).
05 : Ratio-Based Diagnostics

Diagnostic Screening Ratios versus Chemical Receptor Models

Our investigations utilize aerodynamic and volatile tracer ratio metrics to characterize screening regimes:

Diagnostic Ratio Observed Regime Physical / Chemical Indication
PM2.5 / PM10 > 0.65 Post-Monsoon / Winter Fine combustion dominance (biomass burning, vehicular exhaust, secondary aerosols)
PM2.5 / PM10 < 0.40 Pre-Monsoon / Summer Mechanical crustal dust dominance (resuspended road silt, construction dust)
Toluene / Benzene approx 1.5 to 2.2 Highway Corridors Fresh tailpipe vehicular combustion emissions
Toluene / Benzene > 3.0 Industrial Belts Industrial solvent use, printing, chemical processing facilities
Scientific Limitation

These ratios represent screening indicators, not receptor models. Full source apportionment requires chemical speciation filters (carbonaceous aerosols, elemental ions, and trace metals) coupled with EPA Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) or Chemical Mass Balance (CMB) modeling.

06 : Model Reanalysis Parameters

NASA POWER / MERRA-2 Reanalysis Skin Temperature

Analysis 03 evaluates thermal records across the Kanpur grid cell using daily reanalysis data from the NASA POWER project, derived from the MERRA-2 assimilation model:

  • Reanalysis Skin Temperature (TS): Derived from numerical weather reanalysis assimilation on a 0.5° latitude by 0.625° longitude grid (approximately 55 by 60 km). It represents model skin temperature, not satellite radiometer observation and not satellite LST.
  • Winter Daily Mean Difference (Delta T = TS - T2M): A negative winter daily mean difference (TS < T2M, averaging -0.97°C) indicates daily aggregate surface cooling. It does not establish the depth of a nocturnal ground inversion, which requires testing hourly night profiles.
  • Planned Spatial Zoning: TODO[ME]: Spatial thermal zoning High-resolution intra-urban thermal zoning requires satellite thermal sensor imagery (Landsat 8/9 Collection 2 Level 2 at 30 m or MODIS MOD11A1 at 1 km, cloud-masked, with documented zonal means and dates). 07 : Boundary Layer Physics

Temperature Inversion Mechanics and Surface-Air Temperature Coupling

The sharp winter surge in particulate concentrations across the Indo-Gangetic Plain is primarily an atmospheric ventilation problem rather than an emissions surge alone. While seasonal crop residue fires and heating fires add particulate mass, hazardous ground levels occur because the planetary boundary layer collapses vertically.

The Physical Mechanism of Nocturnal Inversion

Under normal daytime conditions, sunlight warms the soil and asphalt. The ground heats the air in contact with it, and because warm air is less dense than the cooler air above, it rises in buoyant convective plumes that carry pollutants several kilometers into the free troposphere.

Between November and January, northern India experiences calm winds (often below 1.5 meters per second) and dry, cloudless skies under regional anticyclonic high pressure. After sunset, the alluvial soil radiates heat into space through longwave infrared emission. The ground cools faster than the air column above, producing a temperature inversion where air temperature increases with height within the lowest 100 to 300 meters. The buoyant stability of this cold ground layer prevents vertical air movement, capping the city beneath an effective thermal ceiling.

Observation vs Mechanism in Our Analysis

In Analysis 03, we document a moderate negative correlation (Pearson r = -0.455) between daily surface skin temperature and ground PM2.5 in Kanpur during the true 308-day overlap window. While this statistical association reflects seasonal cooling and reduced ventilation volume, daily mean reanalysis values cannot measure the vertical temperature gradient (dT/dz) or the exact height of the nocturnal inversion cap. Measuring inversion depth directly requires radiosonde sounding profiles or continuous lidar/ceilometer backscatter measurements.
