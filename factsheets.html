01 : Reporting Briefs & Common Myths

Fast Reference for Science Communicators

During North Indian winter pollution episodes, media coverage often repeats standard technical inaccuracies. These four briefs summarize the underlying statutory rules and physical mechanics with verified scientific citations.

Brief 01 : Regulatory Science

Why the National AQI Is Never an Average

The Misconception: Reporting that an Air Quality Index of 400 represents a combined "average" or "composite blend" of all pollutants in the air.

The Scientific Reality: The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB, 2014) National Air Quality Index uses a strict max-operator rule. For each criteria pollutant with valid data, a piecewise linear sub-index between 0 and 500 is calculated independently. Whichever single pollutant registers the highest sub-index becomes the dominant pollutant, and its exact number becomes the official statutory score for that station:

Headline AQI = max(I_PM2.5, I_PM10, I_NO2, I_SO2, I_CO, I_O3, I_NH3, I_Pb)

Across 1,137 official days at Delhi Alipur, PM2.5 was the dominant pollutant on 88.4% of days (1,005 days), while PM10 governed 11.2% of days and NO2 on 0.4%. The other criteria pollutants virtually never dictate the headline score in winter.

Source: CPCB NAQI Report (October 2014) · Detailed Explainer
Brief 02 : Atmospheric Physics

Thermal Inversion vs. Sudden Emission Spikes

The Misconception: Assuming that late-night pollution spikes (midnight to 07:00 IST) mean factories or trucks are dumping massive new emissions while the city sleeps.

The Scientific Reality: Late-night particulate escalation across the Indo-Gangetic Plain is primarily governed by nocturnal ground radiative cooling. Under clear winter skies, the ground radiates heat into space faster than the air above it, chilling the lowest atmospheric layer. Because cold air is denser than warm air, buoyant convective updrafts cease completely, forming an invisible "inversion lid":

Normal background emissions (cooking fires, overnight heavy diesel bypass traffic, smoldering biomass, brick kilns) are compressed into a shallow boundary layer only a few hundred meters deep. When the effective atmospheric volume shrinks by 80%, particulate concentrations surge 4× to 5× even if gross emission rates remain constant.

Source: Boundary Layer Thermodynamics · Detailed Explainer
Brief 03 : Unit Standards

Physical Concentration (µg/m³) vs. Dimensionless AQI Points

The Misconception: Writing phrases like "pollution reached 450 µg/m³ AQI" or treating concentration and index points as interchangeable.

The Scientific Reality: Mass concentration (µg/m³) is a physical measurement of particulate weight in a cubic meter of air. The AQI is a dimensionless public health category score (0 to 500) translated non-linearly through statutory breakpoints:

Category AQI Scale 24-hr PM2.5 Range 24-hr PM10 Range
Good 0 – 50 0 – 30 µg/m³ 0 – 50 µg/m³
Satisfactory 51 – 100 31 – 60 µg/m³ (NAAQS Standard) 51 – 100 µg/m³
Moderate 101 – 200 61 – 90 µg/m³ 101 – 250 µg/m³
Poor 201 – 300 91 – 120 µg/m³ 251 – 350 µg/m³
Very Poor 301 – 400 121 – 250 µg/m³ 351 – 430 µg/m³
Severe 401 – 500 250+ µg/m³ 430+ µg/m³

Newsroom Rule: State either "the AQI was 420" (no units) or "the 24-hour PM2.5 concentration was 280 µg/m³". Never combine them.

Source: CPCB National Ambient Air Quality Standards (2009) · Breakpoint Methodology
Brief 04 : Quality Assurance

The 16-Hour Quorum Rule and Sensor Hiatuses

The Misconception: Assuming that when an official monitoring station reports "No Data" or goes dark for weeks, government officials are hiding numbers.

The Scientific Reality: Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Stations (CAAQMS) use beta-attenuation monitors (BAM) that run mechanical filter tape. In extremely polluted winter environments, tape rolls tear, beta sources drift, or power supplies fluctuate. CPCB enforces a strict regulatory quorum: a valid statutory daily AQI requires at least 16 valid hourly samples for at least three criteria pollutants, one of which must be particulate matter (PM2.5 or PM10). If a station logs only 15 hours of PM2.5 due to a calibration cycle, no statutory score can be published.

Source: CPCB Data Validation Protocol · Station Registry

03 : Newsroom Verification Checklist

Common Errors to Avoid Before Publishing

  • Avoid Single-Monitor Overreach: A reading from a sensor situated 15 meters from an interstate highway truck terminal (like Delhi Alipur on NH44) reflects local roadside conditions plus regional air masses. Do not publish it as "the average air breathed by 30 million people across the NCR."
  • Distinguish Direct Satellite Observations from Numerical Reanalysis: NASA POWER and GMAO MERRA-2 are physics-based atmospheric model simulations, not satellite photographs or direct ground thermometers. Do not claim a reanalysis dataset measured the asphalt temperature outside someone's house.
  • Differentiate Local Stagnation from Transboundary Smoke: In October and November, northwesterly winds transport agricultural smoke plumes across the Gangetic plain. But once winter cold sets in through December and January, massive particulate spikes are driven by regional boundary layer compression and nocturnal inversions, not continued stubble burning.
  • Acknowledge Missing Historical Data: When plotting multi-year trends, clearly disclose monitoring hiatuses. Smoothing over a 2-year maintenance gap with synthetic straight lines misinforms readers about genuine historical exposure.
