Brief 01 · Kanpur Particulate Dynamics

How much worse is Kanpur’s winter air than its monsoon air?

By Avnish Singh, Civil Engineering undergraduate, HBTU Kanpur October 2026 Layer 1: 3 min read

The Short Answer

In the monitoring records I could analyse, winter PM2.5 in Kanpur was between about 2.4 and 5.1 times the monsoon level. The size of the gap depends on how winter is defined and which monitoring window you use.

The Evidence

Figure 1: Daily PM2.5 Concentrations Across Two Discontinuous Observation Windows
Data Source: CPCB / UPPCB via OpenAQ v3 | Stations: NSI Kalyanpur & Nehru Nagar | n = 1,628 station-days
Reading the chart: Daily particulate records come from two regulatory continuous monitoring stations in Kanpur (NSI Kalyanpur and Nehru Nagar). Data exist across two discrete windows: July 2021 to October 2022 (896 station-days) and February 2025 to August 2026 (732 station-days), totaling 1,628 station-days. Between October 16, 2022 and February 18, 2025, there are 855 days with no public station data. I did not interpolate or fill that hiatus.

What the Data Shows, in Plain Terms

Using standard meteorological definitions (December to February as winter, June to September as monsoon), winter PM2.5 averaged 2.4 times the monsoon baseline across both observation windows (61.4 µg/m³ vs 25.6 µg/m³). In the first window (2021–22), winter ran 2.9 times higher (76.3 vs 26.1 µg/m³); in the second window (2025–26), it was 2.3 times higher (57.4 vs 25.5 µg/m³).

Comparing the peak haze window (November to January) against core monsoon months (July to September) widens the ratio to 3.6 times across pooled data (76.5 vs 21.5 µg/m³). In 2021–22, that peak haze ratio reached 5.1 times (96.1 vs 18.7 µg/m³); in 2025–26, it reached 3.0 times (67.7 vs 22.5 µg/m³). During winter, 48.9% of station-days exceeded India's 24-hour statutory standard of 60 µg/m³ (rising to 68.4% during peak haze), compared to just 3.3% during the monsoon (0.3% in July to September).

What the Data Cannot Show

Two monitoring stations cannot represent the entire urban footprint of a city spanning over 2.7 million residents. Furthermore, the two observation windows are separated by an unmonitored 2.3-year gap (855 days), meaning I cannot determine whether the lower winter average in 2025–26 represents a sustained multi-year trend or normal meteorological variability. Finally, the first window contains no June records, and the second window terminates on August 9, leaving both monsoon baselines partially truncated.

Why It Matters

Annual averages obscure the seasonal extremes that govern real physiological exposure. When nearly seven out of ten days during peak winter exceed statutory air quality limits, population exposure is acutely concentrated into a three-month window. Understanding the exact magnitude of this seasonal multiplier prevents seasonal panic from being mistaken for permanent baseline shifts, while continuous, uninterrupted monitoring would convert these snapshot observations into an accountable long-term trend.
